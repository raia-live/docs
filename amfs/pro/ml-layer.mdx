Every time an agent commits an outcome, it tells SenseLab something useful: this
set of memories was in play, and the work went well — or it didn't. The ML layer
is what SenseLab does with that signal.

It shows up in three ways:

- **Recall gets sharper.** SenseLab learns which kinds of memories tend to precede
  good work, and surfaces those first.
- **Trust gets more accurate.** It checks whether the way it raises and lowers
  confidence matches what actually happened in your data.
- **Your agents become trainable.** The decision traces you've been accumulating
  export as fine-tuning datasets.

Nothing here needs setting up. The models run inside SenseLab and learn from data
you already have, so there is no training infrastructure to stand up and no data
to ship anywhere.

## What it learns from

One thing: your outcome history.

When an agent finishes a task and commits an outcome, SenseLab records which
memories were involved and how the task turned out. That pairing — what the agent
knew, and what happened next — is the entire training signal.

<Warning>
Agents that read and write memories but never commit an outcome produce no signal
at all, and the ML layer has nothing to learn from.

**Failures matter as much as successes.** A history of nothing but good news gives
the models nothing to contrast, so recording only successes makes the ML layer
weaker, not stronger.
</Warning>

## Sharper recall

### The idea

Out of the box, retrieval blends a few generic signals — how closely a memory
matches the query, how recent it is, how confident it is — in fixed proportions.
Sensible defaults, but they're the same for everyone. They can't know that in
*your* store, recency counts for more than confidence, or that the memories which
have been confirmed by real outcomes are the ones that consistently pay off.

Learned ranking replaces that guess with evidence from your own history. Memories
that were in play before successful work become examples of *useful*; memories
that preceded failures — or that nobody has ever validated — become examples of
*not useful*. A model learns the patterns that separate the two, drawing on things
like how confident a memory is, how old it is, how many outcomes have confirmed
it, whether it's a stated fact or a working hypothesis, and whether it's real
knowledge or just a stored file.

That learned judgement then joins retrieval as one more voice, with roughly a
third of the say. The generic signals stay in the mix and make room for it.

### Using it

```
amfs_retrain()
```

Or narrow it to one area of your memory:

```
amfs_retrain(entity_path="checkout-service")
```

You get back a short summary: how many examples it learned from, how well it
performs on examples it wasn't trained on, and which signals turned out to matter
most. That last part is often the interesting one — it's a read on what actually
predicts usefulness in your organisation.

From then on it's automatic. `amfs_retrieve` picks the model up on its own; there
is no flag to set and nothing for your agents to change.

Retraining as your history grows is safe and expected — each run simply replaces
the last.

### Before you have enough history

Learned ranking needs around 20 examples to be worth anything. Below that,
`amfs_retrain` tells you how many it found and stops there. Nothing breaks:
retrieval keeps working exactly as it did before, and you can run it again once
more outcomes have accumulated.

## Better-calibrated trust

### The idea

A memory's confidence is how much trust it has earned. Committing an outcome
adjusts it: success nudges confidence up, failure pulls it down, and failures move
it further than a single success does — trust should be easy to lose and slow to
rebuild.

| Outcome | Effect on confidence |
| :-- | :-- |
| Success | Small increase |
| Minor failure | Small decrease |
| Failure | Larger decrease |
| Critical failure | Largest decrease |

Those are reasonable defaults, but how much a given outcome *should* count depends
on what you do. A critical failure in a payments service doesn't mean the same
thing as one in a logging service.

Calibration looks at your history and asks what actually became of the memories
behind each kind of outcome. Did they keep turning up in failures, or did they go
on to support work that succeeded? From that it reports the adjustments that fit
your data, along with an estimate of how long memories in your store stay useful
before they should be treated as stale.

### Using it

```
amfs_calibrate()
```

To also get figures broken down by area of your memory rather than one set for
everything:

```
amfs_calibrate(per_entity=true)
```

<Note>
Calibration is a report, not a switch. Running it changes nothing — your
confidence values keep updating with the standard adjustments. It tells you what
your data suggests, and whether to act on that is your call. That also makes it
completely safe to run whenever you're curious.
</Note>

Calibration stays quiet until it has enough to go on: it needs at least five
outcomes of a given kind before it will say anything about that kind, and five
outcomes in an area before it will report figures specific to that area. Anything
below the bar comes back at the standard value, unchanged.

## Training better agents

### The idea

A decision trace is already shaped like training data. Here is what the agent
knew, here is what it decided, here is how it turned out — context, action,
result.

Normally, producing that data is the expensive part of fine-tuning, because
someone has to sit down and label it. Your agents generate it as a by-product of
working, and the outcome *is* the label.

Export turns those traces into datasets in three shapes:

| Format | What it captures | What it's for |
| :-- | :-- | :-- |
| SFT | Successful traces: what the agent knew, and what it decided | Teaching a model to imitate decisions that worked |
| DPO | A success and a failure from the same area, side by side | Teaching preference — this, not that — with no human ranking required |
| Reward model | Every memory, scored by how its outcomes turned out | Training a model that can judge quality on its own |

### Using it

```
amfs_export_training_data(format="sft")
```

You get the examples themselves, a count, and how many memories were in scope
altogether — which tells you whether a thin export means thin outcome coverage or
just a narrow filter.

<Tip>
For SFT, SenseLab has to tell the *decision* apart from the background knowledge
around it. It looks for the memory that records the choice — one written under a
key like `decision-retry-policy`, or recorded as an experience.

If your agents don't mark decisions that way, their traces look like all context
and no decision, and SFT export comes back empty. Following the standard memory
conventions gets you this for free.
</Tip>

SenseLab generates the data; you bring the training. The formats line up with the
usual tools:

| Format | Works with |
| :-- | :-- |
| SFT | OpenAI fine-tuning API, Hugging Face `SFTTrainer`, Axolotl |
| DPO | TRL `DPOTrainer`, OpenRLHF |
| Reward model | TRL `RewardTrainer`, custom reward model training |

## What you need before it pays off

| To get | You need |
| :-- | :-- |
| Sharper recall | Around 20 memories linked to outcomes, with a genuine mix of successes and failures |
| Calibrated trust | Five outcomes of a kind; five in an area for area-specific figures |
| A useful export | At least one committed outcome, though real datasets want dozens of traces |

Every one of those counts *outcomes*, not memories. The fastest way to make the ML
layer useful isn't writing more memories — it's making sure the agents already
writing them commit an outcome when they finish, on the bad days as well as the
good ones.

## The loop

```
        agents work, and commit outcomes
                      │
                      ▼
            outcome history builds up
                      │
      ┌───────────────┼───────────────┐
      ▼               ▼               ▼
 sharper recall  calibrated trust  training data
      │                                 │
      └────────► better decisions ◄─────┘
                      │
                      ▼
              more outcome history
```

That's the whole point of it. Agents produce outcome history just by doing their
work; the ML layer turns that history into better recall and better-trained
agents; better recall leads to better decisions, which produce more history. The
only step that takes any discipline is the last one in every task — committing the
outcome.
